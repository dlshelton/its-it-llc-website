Is Your Business Running AI Tools You Don’t Know About? What Southwest Florida Businesses Need to Know

Dennis Shelton November 13, 2026 5 min read Technology

How many AI tools are being used inside your business right now?

Think beyond the subscriptions you approved. Include the chatbot an employee uses to summarize documents, the browser extension someone installed to write emails, and the automation a team member built to organize information.

If you cannot name them all, you have a useful place to start.

Shadow AI is the use of AI tools outside your company’s approved systems and processes. It can leave leadership without a clear picture of what information those tools receive, which accounts control them, or who reviews their work.

For Southwest Florida business owners, responsible AI adoption starts with understanding what is already in use. Before adding more features, establish the rules, access limits, and accountability that help your business use AI with confidence.

How a helpful tool becomes a business dependency

Consider a hypothetical situation: an employee finds an AI tool that turns meeting notes into follow-up emails.

It works well. A coworker starts using it. Eventually, the department relies on it, and someone connects it to a shared workspace to make the process easier.

Leadership sees a smoother workflow. But several questions remain unanswered:

  • Was the tool reviewed before company information was entered?
  • Is it connected through a business account or a personal login?
  • What information can it access?
  • Who checks its output before someone acts on it?
  • Could the business keep using it if the employee left?

The employee may have solved a real problem. That makes the tool worth understanding. A successful demonstration, however, does not answer the questions needed to manage it responsibly.

What counts as shadow AI?

Shadow AI can include a standalone chatbot, an employee-built tool, or an AI feature activated inside software your business already uses. The issue is whether its use falls within your organization’s approval and oversight processes.

The UK’s National Cyber Security Centre describes shadow AI in these terms and recommends understanding employees’ needs so organizations can provide suitable tools and reduce risk. Read its guidance on shadow AI.

Look for activities such as uploading work documents to an unreviewed service, connecting an assistant through a personal account, or building an automation that handles company records without documenting its access.

Start by asking employees what helps them get their work done. That conversation can reveal useful ideas and gaps in your current guidance.

Four questions leadership needs to answer

1. What information reaches the tool?

One tool might receive only text an employee types into a prompt. Another might receive uploaded documents or have an ongoing connection to email or file storage.

Ask your technical team to identify what information each tool receives and what its integrations allow. Review whether that access fits its purpose.

Examine the service’s terms and settings before sharing confidential information. Do not assume every AI service stores, retains, or uses submitted information in the same way.

2. Who controls the account?

A tool used for company work may depend on one employee’s personal login, recovery email, or payment method.

Document who administers it, how access can be removed, and how the business would recover control. Where supported and appropriate, use company-managed accounts and access controls.

3. What can the tool do?

Drafting a suggested response and sending that response automatically have different consequences.

Identify whether a tool can change records, send messages, browse websites, or trigger another process. Decide which actions require approval and who can pause the workflow if something goes wrong.

4. Who checks the result?

Assign a person to review outputs before they affect customers or important business decisions. Define what that involves: checking facts, confirming a record changed correctly, or verifying that a message reached the intended recipient.

A person should remain accountable for the decision and the outcome.

What AI governance means in plain English

AI governance means establishing who can use AI, for which tasks, with which information, and under whose supervision.

For a smaller business, a practical starting point can be straightforward:

  • A list of approved tools and their purposes.
  • Guidance on information employees may share.
  • A named owner for each tool or workflow.
  • A process for reviewing new tools and changes.
  • Human approval for consequential actions.
  • A way to report problems and stop an unsafe workflow.

The National Institute of Standards and Technology’s voluntary AI Risk Management Framework includes maintaining an AI inventory and defining responsibility for managing AI risks. Explore the NIST framework.

You can begin with a manageable review and build from there.

Start with three questions this week

Bring your department leaders and IT provider into the same conversation:

  1. Which AI tools are people using for work, including tools they set up themselves?
  2. What information can each tool receive or access?
  3. Are any connected through personal accounts?

Record the answers in a shared inventory. Include each tool’s purpose, owner, connected accounts, data access, and approval status.

Review tools handling sensitive information or taking action automatically first. Revisit the inventory when employees add tools, enable integrations, or change workflows.

A 10-question AI checklist for business owners

Use these questions to guide your first leadership discussion:

  1. Do we know which AI tools employees use for work?
  2. Does that list include extensions, automations, and AI features within existing software?
  3. Is someone accountable for each tool?
  4. Are accounts and recovery methods under appropriate company control?
  5. Have we documented what information each tool can access?
  6. Have we limited access to what its task requires?
  7. Have we reviewed the service’s handling of our information?
  8. Do employees know which uses and data require approval?
  9. Does a person review important outputs and approve consequential actions?
  10. Can we disable access and keep essential work moving if a tool fails or its creator leaves?

An unanswered question is an action item. Assign someone to investigate it and bring the answer back.
