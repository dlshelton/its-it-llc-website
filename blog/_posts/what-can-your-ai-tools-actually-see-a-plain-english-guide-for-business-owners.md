Your team starts using an AI assistant to summarize documents. It does the job well, so someone connects it to a company workspace to save the trouble of uploading files individually.

Before that connection goes live, there is a question worth asking:

**What information will this tool be able to access—and does it need all of it?**

AI data access depends on the tool’s design, connected accounts, permissions, integrations, and settings. Some tools receive only what a person submits. Others can retrieve information from connected systems or act on it automatically.

For Southwest Florida business owners, understanding those boundaries is an important part of responsible AI adoption. A useful tool deserves an access review that matches the information and responsibilities you give it.

## What a tool needs and what it can reach can be different

Imagine a hypothetical AI assistant built to answer questions about your company’s approved procedures.

It needs access to a folder of current operating instructions. During setup, however, it is connected through an account with access to a much broader collection of business documents.

Depending on how that connection is configured, the assistant could be able to retrieve more information than its job requires.

The software might perform its intended task perfectly. The access still needs attention.

A working demonstration tells you whether a tool can produce a useful result. It does not establish whether the tool’s permissions are appropriate.

## How does AI get access to company information?

There are several routes to review.

### Information employees submit directly

An employee might paste text into a chatbot or upload a document for summarizing. Even without a permanent connection to your systems, the service receives the submitted information.

Ask what employees are allowed to share and whether the service has been reviewed for that use.

### Connections to email, storage, and business software

An assistant may connect to email, shared folders, a customer database, or another application. Its access can depend on the signed-in user, permissions granted to the application, and restrictions within the connector itself.

Ask your technical team to verify the actual configuration. Connecting one folder does not automatically mean every tool can access the whole drive; it also does not prove that access is limited to that folder.

### Local files and automated workflows

A locally running tool may have access through the account or environment it runs under. An automation may also pass information from one service to another.

Review the entire workflow, including intermediate storage, connected services, and outputs. A single screen may show only one part of the process.

## Read access and action permissions deserve separate reviews

Start with what a tool can read. Then ask what it can change.

An assistant that summarizes a document has a different role from one that can edit records, send messages, or delete files. Each additional action deserves a clear business purpose and an appropriate approval process.

For example, you might allow a tool to prepare a customer email while requiring an employee to check and send it. A different workflow might need tighter controls because it changes financial or personnel information.

Write those boundaries down so employees and your IT provider know what has been approved.

## Give each tool only the access its task requires

The security principle behind this is called **least privilege**. NIST describes it as limiting access to the minimum needed to perform assigned work. [Read NIST’s definition of least privilege.](https://csrc.nist.gov/glossary/term/least_privilege)

Applied to AI, a practical review asks:

* Which documents or records does this task require?
* Does the connection grant access beyond those materials?
* Does the tool need to edit information, or is reading enough?
* Who can approve changes to its access?
* How will we test that the restrictions work?

Where supported and appropriate, a dedicated application identity or service account can help separate an automated workflow from an employee’s everyday access. It still needs carefully scoped permissions, secure authentication, and an accountable owner.

Creating another account alone does not solve excessive access.

## Ask where the information goes after the tool reads it

Access is one part of the picture. Processing and storage are another.

A tool may process information locally, send it to an external service, or combine both approaches. Review the actual design rather than assuming that a tool installed on an office computer keeps everything there.

Before approving sensitive information for use, ask:

* Which services receive it?
* What do the applicable terms say about retention and model training?
* Which settings or agreements apply to our account?
* Can we control or delete stored information?
* Does the arrangement fit our customer commitments and business requirements?

The UK’s National Cyber Security Centre warns that sending sensitive information to unapproved consumer AI services can reduce an organization’s visibility and control over it. [Read its shadow AI guidance.](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/blogs/the-hidden-risks-of-shadow-ai)

The answers vary by service and configuration. Review the specific arrangement before sharing information.

## Organize sensitive information before expanding AI access

Ask your team to identify where confidential materials live, who should access them, and whether current sharing permissions reflect those needs.

Pay particular attention to old shared folders, inherited permissions, and files that no longer need broad access.

Classification labels can help identify sensitive information. Where supported, configured controls can use those labels to restrict access or sharing. A label by itself should not be treated as proof that a file cannot leave your environment.

Have your technical team test the controls with mock information before relying on them for sensitive business records.

## An AI access checklist for business owners

For each tool connected to company information, document:

1. Its business purpose and accountable owner.
2. The user or application identity it uses.
3. The files, mailboxes, applications, and records it can access.
4. Whether it can read, edit, send, or delete information.
5. Which outside services receive information.
6. The retention settings and terms that apply.
7. Who approves permission changes and reviews activity.
8. How access can be revoked and the workflow stopped.

Ask your IT provider to demonstrate both an allowed task and a blocked attempt using non-sensitive test data. Keep a record of the results and review access when the workflow changes.

A person should approve the boundaries and make the final call on consequential actions.

## Start with two questions

You do not need to become an AI developer to lead this conversation:

**What information can this tool access?**

**Does it need all of it?**
