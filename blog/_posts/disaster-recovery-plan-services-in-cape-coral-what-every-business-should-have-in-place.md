If your company’s server went down tomorrow morning, how long would it take before your team could work again?

An hour?

A day?

Several days?

For many businesses, the answer isn’t clear until something actually goes wrong.

That’s the problem a well-designed disaster recovery plan is intended to solve.

Cape Coral businesses understand the operational risks that come with hurricanes, power outages, flooding, and internet disruptions. But severe weather isn’t the only event capable of bringing a business to a standstill. Hardware failures, ransomware, cyberattacks, accidental deletion, software problems, and human error can happen on any ordinary business day.

Having your data backed up is important.

Knowing how you’re going to get your business operational again is something different.

That’s disaster recovery.

## What Is a Business Disaster Recovery Plan?

A business disaster recovery plan is a documented strategy for restoring critical technology, data, systems, and business operations following an unexpected disruption.

It identifies what technology your business depends on, how that technology is protected, which systems need to be restored first, and how quickly recovery needs to happen.

A complete disaster recovery strategy may address:

* Servers and workstations
* Business files and databases
* Email and communication systems
* Cloud applications
* Network infrastructure
* Industry-specific software
* Cybersecurity incidents
* Backup systems
* Internet and connectivity
* Employee access to critical technology

The goal isn’t simply to preserve information.

The goal is to minimize downtime and restore the technology your employees need to operate the business.

## Backup and Disaster Recovery Are Not the Same Thing

This is one of the most important distinctions for business owners to understand.

Backup answers the question: Can we recover our data?

Disaster recovery answers the question: Can we recover our business?

You can have perfectly good backups and still experience significant downtime.

Imagine that your company’s primary server fails.

Your files may be safely backed up somewhere else, but your employees still need access to those files. Applications may need to be restored. Hardware may need to be replaced or repaired. Network connections may need to be rebuilt. Permissions may need to be reconfigured.

All of that takes time.

A disaster recovery strategy considers what happens after the failure, not just whether another copy of the data exists.

## What Should a Business Disaster Recovery Plan Include?

Every organization is different, so there isn’t one disaster recovery plan that works for every company.

A 20-person professional office has different technology dependencies than a 200-person organization operating across several locations.

However, there are several components every Cape Coral business should consider.

### 1. Identification of Your Critical Systems

The first step is understanding which technology your company cannot operate without.

That may include your:

* Primary server
* Customer database
* Accounting platform
* Email
* Shared files
* Phone system
* Cloud applications
* Industry-specific software
* Internet connection
* Network infrastructure

Businesses should also determine which systems need to be restored first.

If everything stopped working simultaneously, what would your employees need first to begin serving customers again?

That answer helps establish your recovery priorities.

### 2. Reliable and Monitored Data Backups

Your business should know where its data is backed up, how frequently those backups occur, how long backups are retained, and whether they are being successfully completed.

Backups should also be monitored.

A backup system that quietly stopped working months ago isn’t much help when you suddenly need it.

Equally important, backups should be tested.

The first time you attempt to restore critical business information should not be during an actual emergency.

### 3. A BCER Solution for Rapid Recovery

For businesses that depend heavily on servers, BCER devices can play an important role in a disaster recovery strategy.

A BCER solution creates backups of files, folders, and systems so that critical business technology can be recovered quickly when infrastructure fails.

This becomes particularly important when a physical server experiences a major failure.

Traditional recovery may require replacing hardware, rebuilding the server, restoring data, reconfiguring applications, and reconnecting users before employees can resume normal operations.

Depending on the business environment and the type of incident, a properly configured BCER solution can dramatically shorten that process and may allow a business to resume access to critical systems within minutes rather than waiting for failed infrastructure to be completely rebuilt.

For a company where every hour of downtime affects employees, customers, and revenue, recovery speed matters.

### 4. Defined Recovery Time Objectives

Every business should ask:

How long can we realistically operate without this system?

This is called a Recovery Time Objective, or RTO.

Some systems may be able to remain offline for several hours without significantly affecting the organization.

Others may need to be restored almost immediately.

Understanding those differences helps determine how your disaster recovery environment should be designed.

### 5. Defined Recovery Point Objectives

There’s another important question:

If we had to restore from a backup, how much recent information could we afford to lose?

This is your Recovery Point Objective, or RPO.

For one organization, losing several hours of changes may be manageable.

For another business processing constant transactions or maintaining frequently changing records, losing even a small amount of recent data could create serious problems.

Backup frequency should reflect the actual needs of the business.

### 6. Protection Against Cybersecurity Incidents

Disaster recovery isn’t limited to physical disasters.

Ransomware and other cyberattacks can encrypt files, disable systems, compromise accounts, and prevent employees from accessing the technology they need.

A recovery plan should account for the possibility that the existing technology environment itself has been compromised.

Simply restoring infected systems or compromised data isn’t recovery.

Cybersecurity and disaster recovery therefore need to work together.

Your backup and recovery strategy should be part of a larger security approach that includes monitoring, patching, endpoint protection, access controls, and other appropriate cybersecurity safeguards.

### 7. Protection for Cloud-Based Data

Moving technology to the cloud has changed disaster recovery, but it hasn’t eliminated the need for it.

Businesses increasingly rely on platforms such as Google Workspace, Microsoft 365, cloud-based accounting systems, customer management platforms, and other Software-as-a-Service applications.

It’s easy to assume:

“It’s in the cloud, so it’s backed up.”

That isn’t always the same thing as having the independent backup and recovery strategy your organization needs.

Businesses should understand what their cloud providers protect, how long information can be recovered, and what happens when files are accidentally deleted, accounts are compromised, or information is intentionally removed.

### 8. A Clear Recovery Process

Technology is only one part of disaster recovery.

Someone also needs to know what to do.

A documented recovery plan should establish responsibilities such as:

* Who identifies and reports the problem?
* Who contacts the IT provider?
* Who determines whether disaster recovery procedures should begin?
* Which systems are restored first?
* How are employees notified?
* How will customers be communicated with if necessary?
* How will employees operate while systems are being recovered?

During an emergency, the goal should be to follow a plan—not create one.

### 9. Regular Disaster Recovery Testing

A disaster recovery plan should never be treated as a document that gets created once and forgotten.

Technology changes.

Employees change.

Software changes.

Businesses add new systems, replace servers, move applications to the cloud, and change the way employees work.

Your disaster recovery plan should evolve with those changes.

Testing also helps answer the question that matters most:

Does our recovery strategy actually work?

## How Much Does Business Downtime Actually Cost?

Business owners often begin disaster recovery conversations by asking what a backup or recovery solution costs.

There’s another number worth calculating.

What does downtime cost?

Consider what happens when 25 employees cannot access the technology they need for an entire workday.

You’re still paying salaries.

Customers may still be calling.

Orders may be delayed.

Appointments may need to be rescheduled.

Employees may be unable to access files or systems.

Revenue-producing work may stop.

Management’s attention gets redirected toward the outage.

Then consider the same situation lasting two or three days.

The cost of disaster recovery shouldn’t be evaluated only against the price of technology.

It should also be compared against the financial and operational cost of being unable to do business.

## How Quickly Should Your Business Be Able to Recover?

There isn’t one correct recovery time for every company.

It depends on your systems, infrastructure, applications, business requirements, and the nature of the incident.

That’s why a disaster recovery strategy should be designed around the organization rather than built from a generic checklist.

For some businesses, several hours of downtime may be acceptable.

For others, even one hour creates significant operational consequences.

Technologies such as BCER solutions can dramatically reduce recovery time in the right environment by giving businesses another way to access critical systems while failed infrastructure is being addressed.

The objective isn’t simply to say:

“We have backups.”

It’s to confidently answer:

“Here’s what happens if our systems go down, and here’s how we’re getting the business running again.”

## 10 Questions to Ask Your IT Provider About Disaster Recovery

You don’t need to be an IT expert to start evaluating your company’s disaster recovery readiness.

Ask your IT provider these questions:

1. Where is our critical business data backed up?
2. How frequently are those backups performed?
3. How do we know our backups are completing successfully?
4. When was the last time our backups were tested through an actual restore?
5. What happens if our physical server completely fails?
6. What happens if our company experiences a ransomware attack?
7. How quickly could our employees access critical systems again?
8. How much recent data could we potentially lose during a recovery?
9. Who is responsible for initiating our disaster recovery process?
10. Do we have a documented disaster recovery plan?

Your provider should be able to answer these questions clearly.

If the answers aren’t clear, your disaster recovery strategy may deserve another look.
